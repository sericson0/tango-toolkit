Tango is a walking dance. Almost everything else — <Term id="ocho" />, turns, the
<Term id="cruzada" /> — is a walk that has been bent, delayed, or turned. Which is good news
and bad news: you can start dancing on your first night, and you will still be working on
your walk in twenty years.

<KeyPoints title="The whole page in four lines">

- A step is a **weight transfer**, not a foot placement.
- Arrive fully before you leave again.
- Push the floor away with the standing leg; don't reach with the free one.
- Your upper body tells your partner what is coming.

</KeyPoints>

## Part 1 — Move your weight

Before you go anywhere, get comfortable going nowhere. Stand with your feet together and
move your weight from one foot to the other. That is a complete tango step, minus the travel.

<StepDiagram
  caption="A change of weight. Nothing above the hips should announce it."
  legend={false}
  feet={[
    { role: 'leader', x: 0, y: 0, foot: 'L', label: 'L' },
    { role: 'leader', x: 0.6, y: 0, foot: 'R', label: 'R' },
  ]}
  arrows={[{ from: [0.05, 0.7], to: [0.55, 0.7], label: 'weight', solid: true }]}
/>

What you are looking for: the moment where you could stop, and stay, and nobody would know
you had been mid-anything. If you wobble, you left before you arrived.

<Callout type="tip" title="Test it">
Change weight, then lift the free foot a centimetre off the floor without shifting anything
else. If you have to adjust to do it, you were not fully on the standing leg yet.
</Callout>

Now add a side step, so the feet are apart, and play with where your body sits between them.

<StepDiagram
  caption="A side step. The foot travels, and then the weight follows it."
  legend={false}
  feet={[
    { role: 'leader', x: 0, y: 0, foot: 'L', label: 1 },
    { role: 'leader', x: 0.6, y: 0, foot: 'R', ghost: true },
    { role: 'leader', x: 2, y: 0, foot: 'R', label: 2 },
  ]}
  arrows={[{ from: [0.65, 0.7], to: [1.95, 0.7], label: 'travel' }]}
/>

<Callout type="warning" title="Move your upper body honestly">
As your weight travels between the feet, let the upper body come with it. Sitting halfway
with your chest still over the old foot is the single most common reason a follower cannot
tell what you want. Being over the far foot already **is** the beginning of the next step.
</Callout>

## Part 2 — Add projection

A tango step has a moment before it: the free leg extends, the body leans into the intention,
and only then does the weight go. That gap is where the lead lives, and it is also what makes
the walk look like tango rather than like walking to the shops.

<Accordion title="How to practice projection on your own" tool="hammer" label="Technique" open>

Stand on your left. Send the right foot out along the floor — toe in contact, no weight on it —
until you are at the edge of where you could still pull it back. Hold. Then let the weight go
and arrive.

- Push the floor away with the **standing** leg. The free leg is not reaching, it is being sent.
- Keep the standing knee soft but not bent; think "long", not "locked".
- The foot stays in contact with the floor the whole way out.
- Arrive through the whole foot, not onto the heel.

</Accordion>

<Callout type="source" title="Learned from Alex Krebs">
Consider a small delay before a short step, so the walk stays smooth instead of arriving
early and waiting. Make sure you can transfer your weight *slowly* — if you can only do it
fast, you don't have a choice, you have a fall.
</Callout>

## Walking together

Facing your partner, the two of you walk as one shape. In **parallel system** the leader's
left goes with the follower's right — opposite feet, the default. Change your own weight
without moving your partner and you are in **cross system**, on the same foot, which is where
a lot of the interesting material lives.

<Callout type="question">
Why does the follower's foot land where it lands? Try walking three steps while deliberately
arriving a beat late, and watch what your partner has to do about it.
</Callout>

## Step length

Steps do not all have to be the same size, and a walk made of identical steps sounds like a
metronome rather than like music. A useful sequence to own, from the leader's steps in cross
system on the way to the cross:

<StepDiagram
  caption="Long, collect, short, long, medium — the leader's steps walking to the cross."
  legend={false}
  lanes={[0, 0.75]}
  feet={[
    { role: 'leader', x: 0, y: 0, foot: 'L', label: 1 },
    { role: 'leader', x: 0.75, y: 2.3, foot: 'R', label: 2 },
    { role: 'leader', x: 0, y: 2.6, foot: 'L', label: 3 },
    { role: 'leader', x: 0.75, y: 3.5, foot: 'R', label: 4 },
    { role: 'leader', x: 0, y: 5.4, foot: 'L', label: 5 },
    { role: 'leader', x: 0.75, y: 6.8, foot: 'R', label: 6 },
  ]}
/>

1. Start on the left.
2. **Long** step onto the right.
3. **Change weight** — the left collects and takes over, going nowhere.
4. **Short** step onto the right.
5. **Long** step onto the left.
6. **Medium** step onto the right.

<Callout type="remark">
Steps 2 and 3 are the same beat's worth of music if you want them to be, or two beats if you
want the change of weight to be an event. That choice is musicality, and it is available to
you on every single step.
</Callout>
