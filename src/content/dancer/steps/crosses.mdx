## Classic eight-count basic

**In parallel system:**

<WalkToCrossPreview />

Most people meet the <Term id="cruzada" /> as step five of a memorised eight-count basic, which
is a shame, because the cross is not a step. It is what happens to the follower's legs when
the leader moves in a particular way and does not stop them.

<Callout type="question">
Why is the cross happening? Not "what do I do on five" — why does the follower's foot end up
crossed at all?

<CrossQuestionHint />
</Callout>

## Where the cross comes from

Think about the rectangle made by your shoulders and your hips. It can do three things:

<Accordion title="The shoulders–hips rectangle" tool="ruler" label="Concept" open>

**a) It can move in space.** Translation — you take the whole shape somewhere else.

**b) It can rotate.** The whole rectangle turns about the spine, shoulders and hips together.

**c) It can twist.** Shoulders turn one way, hips stay — this is
<Term id="disassociation">disassociation</Term>.

And then:

- **(a) + (b) create a cross.** You travel and turn as one piece, the follower's free leg runs
  out of room on the open side, and it crosses.
- **(c) in general creates ochos.** The twist sends the free leg around instead of across.

</Accordion>

That is the whole mechanism. If the cross is not appearing, you are usually twisting when you
meant to turn, or turning without travelling.

## Two shapes of cross

There is more than one trajectory a free leg can take to get there, and they feel completely
different to the person doing them.

- The **diagonal** cross: the free foot travels in a straight line to the crossed position.
- The **J** cross: the free foot goes around the standing foot and arrives from the side.

Both exist forwards and backwards, which gives four crosses in total. Play with all of them —
"walk to the cross" can just as easily be walk to the **back** cross.

<Callout type="tip" title="When you lead one, lead the trajectory">
Decide which shape you want the follower's free leg to travel, and lead that, rather than
leading "a cross" and hoping. The difference is legible in the embrace long before the foot
arrives.
</Callout>

## The feet themselves

Standing normally, your feet make a small V — call it the pizza. The cross takes you into the
opposite shape, and that is a bigger change than it looks.

<Accordion title="Technique: the forward cross" tool="screwdriver" label="Technique">

A forward cross is a **backward diagonal linear move** for the crossing leg — it is not a
sideways scoop.

- Use your ankle and calf to get smoothly from the extended foot onto the flat foot.
- Keep the hips straight and facing where they were.
- Activate the adductors — the inner thigh muscles — to bring the free leg in. Imagine
  closing a gap rather than lifting a leg.
- Don't tilt the foot of the free leg.
- Think about where your upper body is balanced. Moving the free leg around corresponds to
  the spine moving the *opposite* way.

</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Technique: the back cross" tool="screwdriver" label="Technique">

A back cross is a **forward linear move** — which surprises people, because it is called a
back cross.

- Keep your hips forward. Do not let them trail behind the crossing leg.
- Keep the standing leg long, and use it the same way you use it for a forward step:
  push the floor away.
- Make sure you are in balance *before* changing weight in the crossed position.
- Bend the standing knee at the very end of the cross, during the change of weight — not
  at the start.

</Accordion>

<Callout type="remark" title="How far around?">
Not far. When the free leg travels "around" the standing leg, it only has to clear your own
other foot. Notice the difference between going around the ball of the foot and going around
the whole foot — that is most of the distance you thought you needed.
</Callout>

<KeyPoints title="If the cross feels bad, check these first">

- Hips forward, not left behind.
- Free foot flat to the floor, not tilted onto its edge.
- Balanced *before* the weight change, not during it.
- Standing knee soft at the end, not braced from the beginning.

</KeyPoints>
