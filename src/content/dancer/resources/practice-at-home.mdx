You need about two metres of floor and a pair of socks. Everything that limits most dancers —
balance, weight transfer, the quality of a single step — can be worked on alone, and is best
worked on alone, because there is nobody to compensate for you.

<KeyPoints title="What solo practice is good for">

- Balance and axis
- Weight transfer and step quality
- Pivots and disassociation
- Listening — genuinely the highest-value thing you can do at home

</KeyPoints>

<Callout type="tip" title="Put music on">
Always. Practising in silence builds a body that moves at its own speed. Practising to
D'Arienzo builds one that moves at the music's speed, which is the entire point.
</Callout>

## The twenty-minute routine

<Exercise
  id="home-routine"
  title="Twenty minutes, one tanda at a time"
  intro="Roughly one tanda per line. Run the whole thing, or pick two."
  items={[
    { text: 'Stand. Both feet, then one foot, eyes closed.', note: 'Two minutes. It is the most boring and most useful thing here.' },
    'Weight changes on the beat, without the head bobbing.',
    { text: 'Slow forward walks across the room and back.', note: 'Arrive completely. Push the floor away.' },
    { text: 'Slow backward walks.', note: 'Reach from the hip, toe in contact with the floor.' },
    'Pivots — both directions, both feet, collected.',
    { text: 'Crosses in place: cross, change weight, cross, change weight.', note: 'Watch the standing knee.' },
    { text: 'Sit down and listen to one full tanda doing nothing else.', note: 'Yes, this counts as practice.' },
  ]}

<Callout type="remark" title="On mirrors">
Useful for checking one specific thing, unhelpful as a permanent habit — you end up dancing
for the mirror instead of feeling what you are doing. Check, then turn away and repeat it
without looking.
</Callout>
