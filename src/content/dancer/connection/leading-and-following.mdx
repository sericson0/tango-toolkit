The two roles get described as though one person decides and the other complies, which is both
wrong and the reason so many people find their role frustrating. Nothing in tango happens
because one body forced another one to move. It happens because two people are standing close
enough to feel the same intention, and both of them act on it.

<KeyPoints title="The short version">

- The lead is an invitation to a place, not a shove towards it.
- The follow is a decision to go, made in your own time, on your own axis.
- Both roles are listening. The difference is what they are listening for.
- If a movement needs force to happen, it was not led — it was performed on someone.
- Silence is legal. Standing still together is a perfectly good thing to do with eight beats.

</KeyPoints>

## What the lead is actually doing

Leading is the job of being clear about where you are going and arriving there yourself. Your
chest, your axis, and your feet all say the same thing at the same time, early enough to be
felt and slowly enough to be answered.

Almost every lead that does not work is one of three things: it was late, it was ambiguous, or
it was two instructions at once. None of those are fixed by adding pressure.

**What good looks like:** your partner moves before you have finished asking. You never have to
repeat yourself, and you never have to correct anyone mid-step.

<Callout type="warning" title="The arms are not the lead">
Arms hold a shape. The moment they start doing the steering — pulling on a back, twisting a
shoulder, squeezing to indicate a pivot — your partner has to spend the whole dance defending
their own balance. Whatever you were trying to lead has already failed by then; the arm is
just how you find out.
</Callout>

## What the follow is actually doing

Following is not waiting to be told. It is arriving completely on each step, staying on your
own axis, and moving the instant something is offered — with your full weight, in your own
timing, at the size you can actually control.

The information you are answering is small and it is early. Most of what feels like "I could
not tell what they wanted" is really "I was still finishing the last step".

**What good looks like:** you complete every step without being asked to, you are not braced
against anything, and your partner never feels you deciding.

<Callout type="remark" title="A follower who arrives changes everything">
The single biggest upgrade available to a follower is completing each step — full weight, on
balance, done — rather than hovering ready for the next one. It gives the leader something
real to lead from, and it makes the pauses feel deliberate instead of empty.
</Callout>

## The part nobody says out loud

Both roles are responsible for the connection, and both roles can break it. A leader can
overwhelm; a follower can anticipate. The tell is the same in both directions: someone is
arriving somewhere before the other person got there.

<Callout type="tip" title="Anticipating is not helping">
Guessing correctly and being led are indistinguishable to you and completely different to your
partner. If you find yourself right most of the time, try being late on purpose for one
song — a real lead will still be there when you get there.
</Callout>

<Accordion title="Common mismatches, and what actually causes them" tool="gears" label="Diagnosis">

**"They keep losing me."** Usually the lead is late rather than weak. Start the intention
earlier and let the step take as long as it takes.

**"I never know what they want."** Usually two signals at once — a turn and a weight change,
or a change of direction started before the last step landed. One thing at a time.

**"It feels like being pushed around."** The steps are bigger than the shared balance can
carry. Halve the size; nothing else needs to change.

**"They are always ahead of me."** Anticipation, and it is often a habit from a class where
the same sequence got repeated forty times. Dance something unfamiliar and it goes away.

**"It works with my teacher and nobody else."** Real, and normal. Teachers are excellent at
receiving unclear information. Dancing with other people is how you find out what you were
actually sending.

</Accordion>

## Learning the other role

Even a little of the opposite role is the fastest information you will ever get about your own.
Leaders discover that half of what they thought was a lead is unreadable; followers discover
how much a braced arm or an unfinished step costs the person opposite. An hour of it at a
<Term id="practica" /> is worth a month of being told.

<Callout type="question">
Next time something does not work, ask your partner what it felt like rather than telling them
what happened. The answer is usually somewhere neither of you was looking.
</Callout>
