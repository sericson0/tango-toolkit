Everything you lead and everything you follow travels through the <Term id="abrazo" />. It is
also the part of tango that people are most anxious about and least often taught explicitly —
you get shown where to put your hands in the first ten minutes of your first class, and then
usually never again.

<KeyPoints title="The short version">

- The embrace is offered, not taken. Wait for it to be accepted.
- Arms hold a shape; they do not squeeze and they do not go limp.
- Both people stand on their own feet. An embrace is not a place to put your weight.
- Open or close it as the floor requires — a crowded milonga is not the place for an open embrace.
- If something hurts, change it. Nobody wants to be danced with by someone in pain.

</KeyPoints>

## How to offer one

Walk up, stand close enough that you are already sharing the space, and wait half a second.
That pause is not politeness for its own sake — it is how the other person tells you how close
they want to be, whether they want their head anywhere near yours, and how much of their weight
they are comfortable sharing. People will answer clearly if you leave them room to.

Then arrive somewhere and stay there. Most embraces that feel unpleasant are not too tight or
too loose; they are moving. A hand that keeps resettling, an arm that creeps up a back
mid-song, a chest that advances and retreats — all of it reads as fidgeting to the person
inside it.

<Callout type="tip" title="Match, do not impose">
Take the embrace you are offered rather than the one you prefer. Someone dancing further away
than you like is telling you something, and closing the gap yourself is the fastest way to make
a stranger regret the tanda.
</Callout>

## Close, open, and the space between

Close embrace shares contact through the chest; open embrace holds a frame with space between
you. They are not beginner and advanced versions of each other — they are different tools, and
most dancers use both inside a single song.

The floor usually decides. A packed milonga has no room for the shapes an open embrace invites,
and dancing open in one is how people get kicked. A quiet practica has room for either.

<Callout type="remark" title="Close embrace is not tighter">
It is closer, which is a different thing. Contact through the chest, with both people still
standing entirely on their own feet. A close embrace that involves gripping is just an open
embrace being held hostage.
</Callout>

<Accordion title="What usually goes wrong" tool="spanner" label="Diagnosis">

**Leaning on each other.** Comfortable for about a minute, then exhausting for both, and it
makes every pivot impossible. Take your own weight — see [Posture & Axis](/dancer/steps/posture/).

**The squeezed arm.** Steering with the arms instead of the chest. Your partner spends the song
defending their balance rather than listening.

**The collapsed arm.** The opposite, and just as unhelpful: nothing to receive, so nothing gets
through. Hold a shape without holding on.

**Shoulders up around the ears.** Almost always nerves. It travels straight into your partner
and it will not go away by being told about it. Exhale.

**The hand that wanders.** Put it somewhere and leave it there for the whole song. Anywhere
reasonable is fine; moving is not.

**Heads.** Where a head goes is negotiated, never assumed. If you are unsure, do not.

</Accordion>

## Adjusting mid-dance

Embraces get adjusted constantly, and it is not rude to do it — it is rude to endure something
painful and then avoid someone for a year. Do it between songs where possible, or in a pause,
and do it in a way the other person can feel coming. Nobody is offended by a small
repositioning; plenty of people are quietly hurt by a wrist held wrong for twelve minutes.

<Callout type="warning" title="Pain is information, not an inconvenience">
If your shoulder, neck, or lower back hurts while dancing, stop and change something. Tango
does not require enduring anything, and dancers who work through it tend to be dancers who
stop dancing.
</Callout>

<Callout type="question">
Ask a partner you trust: what does my embrace feel like? Not "is it okay" — that gets you a
polite yes. Ask what it feels like, and let them take their time answering.
</Callout>
