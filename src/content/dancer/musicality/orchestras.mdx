You do not need to identify orchestras by ear to enjoy a milonga. But the moment you can hear
*who* is playing, you stop being surprised by the music — you know roughly what the next four
minutes will ask of you before the first phrase is over.

## The big four, from the floor

<Accordion title="Juan D'Arienzo — the beat" tool="sliders" label="Rhythmic" open>

Fast, insistent, unmistakable. Called *El Rey del Compás* — the King of the Beat — and it is
not marketing. If you want to practice being exactly on the pulse, this is your orchestra.

**Asks you for:** precision, energy, clean weight changes. **Does not ask for:** brooding.

</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Carlos Di Sarli — the line" tool="sliders" label="Lyrical">

Smooth, wide, elegant. The beat is there but it is carried rather than hammered. Long walks
live here.

**Asks you for:** a long, calm walk and good posture. **Punishes:** rushing.

</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Aníbal Troilo — the balance" tool="sliders" label="Both">

Rhythmic when it wants to be, achingly lyrical when it wants to be, often in the same track.
The orchestra that most rewards actually listening rather than assuming.

**Asks you for:** attention, and a willingness to change your mind mid-song.

</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Osvaldo Pugliese — the drama" tool="sliders" label="Advanced">

Enormous dynamic swings, deliberate tension and release, passages where the pulse almost
disappears and then slams back. Wonderful, and not where to start.

**Asks you for:** patience, floor craft, and a partner you know.

</Accordion>

## Beyond the four

Biagi (piano fireworks), Tanturi, Rodríguez and Canaro (dependable and danceable), Caló and
Demare (lush and singer-led), Laurenz, De Angelis, Fresedo, Donato — each with a sound worth
learning. The discographies have every recording, with the recording studio, technology, and
era groupings that explain why two tracks by the same orchestra can feel like different bands.
