Almost everyone under-practices and over-classes. A class hands you information at a rate you
cannot absorb; practice is the slow part where a few of those things actually get into your
body. Twenty minutes, three times a week, beats a four-hour workshop.

<KeyPoints title="The principles">

- Practice one thing at a time, badly, slowly, on purpose.
- Slow is harder than fast. If it is easy, go slower.
- Fix the cause, not the symptom. A bad cross is usually a balance problem.
- Stop while it is still going well.

</KeyPoints>

## Classes, practicas, milongas

They are three different activities and it helps to be clear about which one you are in.

<Accordion title="What each one is for" tool="toolbox" label="Orientation" open>

**Class.** New information. Expect to be bad at it. Do not expect to leave able to do it.

**Practica.** Repetition and experiment. You can stop, talk, try it four different ways, ask
your partner what they felt. This is where improvement actually happens, and it is the most
under-attended event in every tango community.

**Milonga.** Dancing, not practising. Do what you can already do, well. Trying to debug a
sequence on a crowded social floor is unfair to your partner and to everyone around you.

</Accordion>

<Callout type="tip" title="Take one thing away">
At the end of every class, decide the single thing you are taking away and write it down. One
sentence. If you cannot name it, you did not learn it, and it will be gone by Thursday.
</Callout>

## Practising with a partner

<Callout type="warning" title="Do not teach each other">
Unless one of you actually is the teacher. Instead, describe what you *felt* — "I lost my
balance there", "I could not tell which direction you wanted" — and let the other person work
out what to change. Diagnosis from your partner is information; instruction from your partner
is usually noise.
</Callout>
