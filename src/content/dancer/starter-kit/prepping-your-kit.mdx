Learning to dance tango takes time and practice. Just like how you wouldn't 
expect to get stronger without spending time working out, you can't expect to
become a smooth dancer without classes, practice, and hours on the dance floor. 
But not all time spent is of equal value. Here are a few useful tools for planning
how to spend your time to get the most out of the time you spend.

<KeyPoints title="The short version">

- The core kit is four things: a **weekly class**, a **practica**, **tango in your ears**, and
  **ten minutes at home**. Everything else is a supplement.
- **Local beats online.** No video replaces a live teacher and a real partner.
- **Listening is the highest-return hour you have.** It is free, portable, and it compounds.
- **Consistency beats intensity.** One class a week, every week, outruns any binge.

</KeyPoints>

## Shop, cook, eat

This page is called *prepping* for a reason: spending your tango time well works a lot like
feeding yourself. Every hour you give the dance is doing one of three jobs.

**Shopping is gathering the ingredients.** Information coming in: classes, workshops, videos,
articles, watching better dancers across the floor. Necessary, abundant, and the easy part —
someone hands you things and you carry them home.

**Cooking is preparing what you brought home.** Practice and study: the practica, the drills in
the kitchen (literally), the review after class, the focused listening. This is where
ingredients turn into something you can actually serve — where information becomes dancing.

**Eating is enjoying the fruits of your labor.** The point of the whole exercise: dancing at
milongas, listening for pleasure, the community around the floor. Not a reward you have to earn
first, either — eating is also how you find out what is worth shopping for next.

The analogy earns its keep in two places. First: you need all three, every week. Shopping
without cooking fills the fridge with things that rot — notes never revisited, figures seen
once and never danceable. Cooking without eating turns a social dance into homework. And eating
alone empties the kitchen fast. Second: beginners systematically over-shop and under-cook.
Information is easy to acquire and feels like progress, but it is not dancing yet — when your
dancing feels stuck, the fix is rarely at the store. It is almost always in the kitchen.

<Callout type="remark" title="The mix shifts over the years">
In your first year, the sensible split is heavy on shopping and cooking, with regular tastes of
eating so you remember why you are doing this. A decade in, it inverts: mostly eating, enough
cooking to stay sharp, the occasional shopping trip when something has gone stale. Trouble
usually looks like running someone else's ratio — the beginner who only eats, or the ten-year
dancer who never stopped shopping.
</Callout>

## The irreplaceable resources are local

Tango is transmitted person to person, and the resources that matter most are the ones within
driving distance. In rough order of importance:

**A regular class.** One good weekly class, attended reliably, is the backbone of everything.
Not the best class in the world — the good-enough class you will actually keep going to.
[Where Should I Start?](/dancer/starter-kit/where-to-start/) has advice on choosing one; the
short version is pick a teacher who explains *why*, and stay put for a while.

**A <Term id="practica" />.** Class hands you information faster than you can absorb it; the
practica is where any of it gets into your body. You can stop, talk, repeat things, and ask
your partner what something felt like. If your scene has one, it is worth more than a second
class. If it does not, a corner of floor and one willing partner after class is a practica.

**A milonga.** Not yet as a place to dance — as a place to watch, listen, and see what the
thing you are learning actually looks like in the wild. An hour of watching a crowded floor
teaches you what all those classes are for. The
[Milonga Survival Guide](/dancer/starter-kit/milonga-survival-guide/) covers the conventions
before you go.

**The people.** Every scene has organisers, DJs, and dancers who remember being new and are
glad to be asked. The fastest way to find out what is on, which classes suit beginners, and
where the practica moved to this month is to ask any of them.

<Callout type="tip" title="Finding your scene">
Search "*your city* tango" and you will nearly always find a local calendar, a Facebook group,
or a school site listing everything on offer. One message to whoever runs it — "I'm brand new,
where should I start?" — will get you a better answer than an evening of browsing. Every scene
is quietly delighted when a beginner appears.
</Callout>

## The music is the free resource

Of everything in the kit, music is the only resource with no gatekeeper, no schedule, and no
cost — and it is the pillar that pays off longest. Dancers who listen between classes hear the
beat sooner, stop counting sooner, and enjoy milongas sooner, because nothing on the floor
sounds like a stranger.

Start a playlist of Golden Age orchestras — D'Arienzo, Di Sarli, Troilo, Canaro will carry you
for months — and put it where your commute is. No study required; volume of exposure does the
work at this stage.

- [Knowing the Orchestras](/dancer/musicality/orchestras/) — who you are hearing, and how to tell them apart.
- [Discographies](/discographies/) — every recording, by orchestra, singer, and year, when you want more of something.
- [Name That Tango](/name-that-tango/) — a game that tests whether you can really tell the orchestras apart yet.

<Callout type="warning" title="A word about YouTube">
There is more tango on YouTube than you could watch in a lifetime, and almost none of it will
make you a better dancer in month three. Watching is not learning — tango is a dance of feel,
and feel does not transmit through a screen. Use video to *review* what a class already put in
your body, or to enjoy watching great dancers. As a curriculum, it mostly produces people who
can describe movements they cannot do.
</Callout>

## How to spend your hours

A realistic beginner has perhaps four to six hours a week for tango. How those hours are
divided matters more than how many there are — two dancers with identical schedules can be a
year apart by month twelve purely on allocation. In meal terms: the week needs all three of
shopping, cooking, and eating in it, and the template below is one way to plate that.

<Accordion title="A week that compounds" tool="toolbox" label="The template" open>

**One class, weekly.** Sixty to ninety minutes. The same class, the same teacher, every week.
This is the load-bearing hour; protect it before all others.

**One practica, or practice hour.** Whatever the class gave you, slowly, without the pressure
of keeping up. This hour roughly doubles the value of the class hour.

**Ten minutes at home, most days.** Weight changes, standing on one leg, slow walking. It
sounds like nothing. It is the difference between month six and month twelve.
[Practice at Home](/dancer/resources/practice-at-home/) has the routine.

**Listening, whenever life allows.** Commute, cooking, walking. This costs you no dedicated
time at all, which is why skipping it is such a strange economy.

**A milonga, monthly-ish.** To watch, to listen, to stay an hour. Dancing there comes when it
comes; being in the room pays immediately.

</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Where beginner hours quietly disappear" tool="toolbox" label="The traps">

Most of these are the same mistake in different clothes: shopping dressed up as cooking.

**Bingeing figure videos.** Feels like study, produces nothing your body can use, and eats the
hours that walking practice needed. One video reviewing what class covered is plenty.

**Workshop-hopping.** Intensive weekends feel like fast-forward, but material you cannot yet
absorb does not stick. Until the basics are comfortable, a weekend workshop is mostly an
expensive way to feel briefly behind. Regularity is the fast lane.

**Gear before balance.** Proper tango shoes are lovely and can wait. Smooth soles that stay on
your feet are the entire requirement for months — spend the shopping hours on the walk.

**Reading the arguments.** Every style debate, embrace controversy, and forum war will still be
there in three years, when you will have the dancing to actually have an opinion. None of it
improves a single step now.

</Accordion>

<Callout type="note" title="The honest math">
Call it five hours a week. Class takes one and a half. The difference between dancers a year
from now is rarely talent, and it is not the class — everyone took the class. It is what the
other three and a half hours did: music in the ears, minutes on one foot in the kitchen, a
practica instead of a second helping of YouTube.
</Callout>

<Callout type="question">
Look at your kit as it stands today. Which piece is missing — the class, the practica, the
music habit, or the ten minutes at home? Whichever one you just thought of is the thing to fix
this week, and it is probably easier to fix than you think.
</Callout>
