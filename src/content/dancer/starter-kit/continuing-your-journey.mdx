If you have got this far, your kit is stocked, you know what you are building, what the
movements are called, and how to get through a night without embarrassing yourself. That is the whole starter
kit. What follows is the much longer part, and it has no syllabus — so here is a map of where to
go next, both inside this toolkit and out in the world.

<KeyPoints title="The next year in four lines">

- Keep one weekly class, and add a practica. Consistency still beats intensity.
- Deepen the music. It is the pillar that pays off most between year one and year three.
- Dance with strangers, in other rooms, in other cities.
- Record yourself once. It is unpleasant and it is worth more than ten classes.

</KeyPoints>

## Getting past the first plateau

Somewhere between six months and two years, most people hit a wall: you are no longer a
beginner, the beginner class is no longer teaching you anything, and you are not visibly
improving. It is the most common point at which people quit, and it is nearly always a sign
that the *way* you are learning needs to change rather than the amount.

<Accordion title="What actually breaks the plateau" tool="toolbox" label="Practical" open>

**Take a private lesson.** One good private is worth a term of group classes at this stage,
because a teacher can finally address *your* specific habit instead of the room's average.
Bring a question, not a request for material.

**Go back to the walk.** It sounds like a punishment and it is the single most reliable
unblocker. Almost everything that feels stuck at eighteen months is a weight-transfer problem
you got away with at six.

**Change partners deliberately.** If you always dance with the same people, you have learned to
dance with *them*. Strangers are the honest test.

**Learn the other role.** At this stage it stops being a curiosity and becomes the fastest
diagnostic tool you have.

**Record yourself.** A phone on a chair for one tanda. You will hate it and you will fix three
things immediately that no amount of being told would have fixed.

**Take a workshop with a visiting teacher.** Not for the material — for the second opinion, in
different language, on something you thought you understood.

</Accordion>

<Callout type="warning" title="The most common wrong fix">
Adding more figures. When progress stalls, the instinct is to learn new material, because it
feels like doing something. It reliably produces a dancer with a large vocabulary and an
unpleasant embrace. Go back down a pillar instead — body, connection, music, floor.
</Callout>

## Beyond your home milonga

<Accordion title="Festivals, marathons, and encuentros" tool="toolbox" label="Where to go">

**Festivals.** Classes and workshops during the day, milongas at night, usually with visiting
teachers and performances. The best on-ramp for a newer dancer — there is structure, and you are
expected not to know anyone.

**Marathons.** No classes, no performances, just milongas running most of the day and night.
Aimed at dancers who are comfortable socially, and a very fast way to accumulate experience.

**Encuentros.** Smaller, close-embrace-focused, strongly traditional in their <Term
id="codigos">códigos</Term> — balanced roles, cabeceo throughout, seated by role. Wonderful, and
worth waiting until you are confident before your first.

**Travelling.** Tango exists in almost every city on earth, and visiting a milonga where you know
nobody is the single most instructive night you can have. Look up the local schedule, arrive
early, sit visibly, and be pleasant. It works everywhere.

**Buenos Aires.** Eventually. There is no substitute, and the two-week version of it will change
how you hear the music.

</Accordion>

<Callout type="tip" title="Dance with people better than you — and worse">
Better dancers show you what is possible and quietly fix things you did not know were broken.
Newer dancers are the honest audit: if a beginner can follow you, your lead is clear. A diet of
only one or the other stalls you in different ways.
</Callout>

## Keep listening

Of all the things that separate a two-year dancer from a ten-year dancer, the largest by some
distance is what they hear. You can improve at that on a bus, in a kitchen, without a partner,
without a floor, and without a single class — which makes it the best return on time available
to you.

Put a tango playlist on for the commute. Learn to recognise four orchestras. Then eight. Then
notice which singer is which. It compounds quietly for years.

<Callout type="question">
What would you like to be able to do in a year that you cannot do now? Write it down as one
sentence. Almost nobody does this, and it is the difference between six years of tango and one
year of tango repeated six times.
</Callout>
