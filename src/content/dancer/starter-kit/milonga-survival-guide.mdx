A <Term id="milonga" /> is a social tango dance, and it runs on conventions that nobody
announces. The <Term id="codigos">códigos</Term> are not snobbery — they exist so that a room
full of strangers can dance together without negotiating everything out loud. Here is the whole
system, plus the parts people are actually anxious about.

<KeyPoints title="If you remember five things">

- Music comes in **tandas** of three or four songs. You dance the whole tanda with one person.
- The **cortina** between tandas means: thank your partner, clear the floor.
- Invitations happen by **eye contact**, from your seat, before anyone stands up.
- Travel **counter-clockwise**, never overtake, dance smaller when it is crowded.
- **Watching is legitimate.** Nobody is judging the person sitting down.

</KeyPoints>

## Before you go

<Accordion title="What to bring, when to arrive" tool="toolbox" label="Practical" open>

**Shoes.** Smooth-soled and secure on your foot. Carry them and change there — outdoor soles
bring grit onto the floor and grit ruins pivots for everyone.

**A spare shirt.** If you sweat, this is not optional, and everyone will silently thank you.
Deodorant, and go easy on strong scent — you will be dancing cheek to cheek with strangers.

**Water, and cash.** Many milongas take cash at the door only.

**Mints.** Same reasoning as the shirt.

**When to arrive.** Early is quieter, more forgiving, and better for beginners — smaller floor,
easier invitations, and the DJ tends to be playing gentler tandas. Peak hour is the hardest
floor to navigate and the hardest time to get dances.

**Where to sit.** Somewhere you can see and be seen. Sitting behind a pillar or facing a wall
makes you invisible to invitations, and this is the single most common reason a beginner spends
their first milonga not dancing.

</Accordion>

## How the night is organised

Music comes in <Term id="tanda">tandas</Term>: three or four songs by the same orchestra, in
the same style. You dance a whole tanda with the same person. Between tandas comes a
<Term id="cortina" /> — a short burst of non-tango music, often something deliberately
un-danceable. The cortina means: thank your partner, and clear the floor.

Tandas rotate through the three rhythms across the night, usually something like tango, tango,
vals, tango, tango, milonga. Once you notice the pattern, you can predict what is coming and
save yourself for the orchestras you love.

<Callout type="tip" title="Why it works">
The cortina is a graceful exit built into the evening. Nobody has to extract themselves from
anything — the music does it for you. This is why walking off mid-tanda is a strong signal, and
why you should not do it unless something is genuinely wrong.
</Callout>

<Callout type="remark" title="Listen to the first song before committing">
The first track of a tanda tells you the orchestra, the era, and the mood. Experienced dancers
invite *after* it starts for exactly this reason — you are choosing a partner and a piece of
music at the same time.
</Callout>

## Asking, and being asked

Most milongas use the <Term id="cabeceo" />: you invite someone with eye contact and a nod,
from where you are sitting, and they accept with a nod or decline by looking away. It depends
on the <Term id="mirada" /> — the look that makes it possible — so both people are doing
something, and a follower who wants to dance is not passive.

<Accordion title="How the cabeceo actually works" tool="toolbox" label="How to" open>

1. Wait for the tanda to start so everyone knows what is playing.
2. Look around the room. Find the person you want to dance with.
3. Catch their eye and hold it for a moment. Raise your eyebrows, tilt your head.
4. If they nod back, *then* stand up and walk over.
5. If they look away, look somewhere else. That is the entire refusal, and it costs nobody
   anything.

The genius of it is that a decline is invisible. Nobody has been rejected in front of the
room, and nobody had to say no out loud.

</Accordion>

<Callout type="warning" title="Common beginner mistakes">
Walking across the floor to ask someone in person — it puts them on the spot in a way the
cabeceo is specifically designed to avoid. And standing up *before* the nod, which means you
are now walking towards someone who has not agreed to anything.
</Callout>

<Callout type="note" title="Not every room does it">
Some communities invite verbally, some are mixed, and some use cabeceo only at peak hour. Ten
minutes of watching tells you which room you are in. When in doubt, follow what everyone else
is doing.
</Callout>

## On the floor

<KeyPoints title="Floor craft">

- Travel counter-clockwise along the <Term id="line-of-dance" />. Everybody, always.
- Do not overtake. If the couple ahead is slow, dance smaller.
- Do not back up into the person behind you.
- Leaders: you are responsible for what is behind your partner. They cannot see it.
- Keep steps small and low when it is crowded. Save the big movements for an empty floor.
- If you bump someone, make eye contact and acknowledge it. That is all it takes.

</KeyPoints>

<Callout type="remark" title="Lanes">
Busy milongas form lanes — an outer lane that travels steadily and an inner one that is usually
slower and more crowded. Pick a lane and stay in it. Wandering between lanes is how collisions
happen.
</Callout>

**Entering the floor.** Wait at the edge, catch the eye of the leader coming round, and let them
wave you in. Stepping into the <Term id="ronda" /> without looking is the tango equivalent of
pulling out of a side road without indicating.

**Leaving the floor.** Wait for the cortina, then walk off through the middle rather than
against the traffic.

## When it goes wrong

Everything in this section happens to everyone. None of it is a disaster.

<Accordion title="I forgot everything mid-dance" tool="toolbox" label="It happens">

Walk. Seriously — go back to walking on the beat until something comes back to you. A tanda of
calm walking is a genuinely good dance and nobody in the room will notice anything went wrong.
Panicking and reaching for a half-remembered sequence is what actually spoils it.

</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Nobody is asking me to dance" tool="toolbox" label="It happens">

Usually one of four things, all fixable: you are sitting where nobody can see you; you are
looking at your phone during the invitation window between the cortina and the first song; you
are new and nobody knows you yet; or the room is at peak hour and everyone already has plans.

What works: arrive early, sit visibly, look up and around during cortinas, and go back to the
same milonga a few times. Regularity converts strangers into people who dance with you far
faster than skill does.

</Accordion>

<Accordion title="I collided with someone" tool="toolbox" label="It happens">

Make eye contact, nod or say sorry, carry on. Do not stop to discuss it, do not spend the rest
of the tanda apologising, and if it was not your fault, still nod. Collisions are usually
shared and always survivable.

</Accordion>

<Accordion title="I want to stop mid-tanda" tool="toolbox" label="It happens">

You are allowed. "Thank you, I need to sit down" is complete and requires no reason, and any
decent partner will walk you off the floor without comment. It is a strong signal, so it is not
for a merely uninspiring dance — but for anything that hurts, is unsafe, or makes you
uncomfortable, use it without hesitation and without guilt.

</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Someone far better than me invited me" tool="toolbox" label="It happens">

Accept. They invited you, which means they already know roughly what they are getting and want
to dance anyway. Dance simply and stay on the beat. Good dancers are usually delighted by a
beginner who does three things well and is not trying to do nine.

</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Someone is holding me too tightly, or hurting me" tool="toolbox" label="It happens">

Adjust first — physically open your frame slightly, or use a hand to make space. Most people
respond immediately without a word being said. If it does not change, say it plainly: "could
you hold a little lighter?" If it still does not change, end the dance. You never owe anyone the
rest of a tanda.

</Accordion>

## Small things that matter

- Arrive having showered. Bring a spare shirt if you sweat.
- Thank your partner at the end of the tanda. Walk them off the floor if that is the local habit.
- If you are learning, say so. Nobody minds a beginner who is calm and on the beat.
- Do not teach on the social floor. Not your partner, not anyone. Save it for the practica.
- Do not talk through the tanda. A word between songs is normal; a conversation is not.
- Watch for a while before you dance. It is the fastest way to learn what this particular room does.

<Callout type="question">
Every milonga has slightly different customs. What does *this* room do — cabeceo or verbal
invitation, three-song or four-song tandas, does anybody sit out the cortina? Ten minutes of
watching answers all of it.
</Callout>
