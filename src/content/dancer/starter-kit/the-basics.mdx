Tango's vocabulary is much smaller than it looks from the outside. Nearly everything you will
see on a social floor is one of six or seven things, combined in a different order, at a
different speed, in a different direction. This page is the map — a paragraph on each, and a
link to the page that teaches it properly.

<KeyPoints title="The whole vocabulary, honestly">

- **Walk** forwards, backwards, or to the side.
- **Pause**, and change weight without going anywhere.
- **Pivot**, which turns a walk into an ocho.
- **Cross**, where one foot passes the other and takes the weight.
- **Turn** around a shared centre.

That is the lot. Everything with a fancy name is a combination of those five.

</KeyPoints>

## Before any movement: how you stand and how you hold

Two things come before the first step, and both outlive every figure you will ever learn.

Your <Term id="axis" /> is the vertical line you balance on. On your own axis you could stand
there all night without your partner; off it, every step is a small rescue that they can feel.
The <Term id="abrazo" /> is the frame you share — comfortable for both of you, held rather than
clamped, and the channel every lead and every response travels through.

→ [Posture & Axis](/dancer/steps/posture/) · [The Embrace](/dancer/steps/embrace/)

## The walk

Tango is a walking dance. The leader steps forward, the follower steps back, and the two of you
travel together along the <Term id="line-of-dance" />. It sounds trivial and it is the work of
a lifetime: complete steps, weight fully transferred, arriving *on* the beat rather than
somewhere near it, with the chest leading and the feet catching up.

Most of the time you are in <Term id="parallel-system" /> — opposite feet, so when the leader
steps with the left, the follower steps with the right. Change weight without moving your
partner and you are in <Term id="cross-system" />, which is where a lot of tango lives.

→ [The Walk](/dancer/steps/walk/)

## The pause

Standing still, in time, on purpose. Beginners treat pauses as gaps to be filled; experienced
dancers treat them as the point. A pause is also where a weight change happens — shifting from
one foot to the other without travelling anywhere, which quietly changes everything available
to you next.

<Callout type="tip" title="The cheapest upgrade in tango">
Take one deliberate pause per song, on a phrase ending, and hold it a beat longer than feels
comfortable. It costs no technique and it makes the dance feel finished rather than continuous.
</Callout>

## The cross

The <Term id="cruzada" /> is where the follower's free foot crosses in front of the standing
leg and takes the weight. It is not a step you are told to do — it is what naturally happens
when the walk is set up a certain way, which is why it is worth understanding rather than
memorising. It shows up constantly, and it is a lovely place to arrive on the end of a phrase.

→ [Crosses](/dancer/steps/crosses/)

## Ochos

An <Term id="ocho" /> is a step followed by a pivot, repeated — forwards or backwards, tracing
a figure eight on the floor. The eight is a by-product, not a goal. The half everyone skips is
the pivot, which is powered by <Term id="disassociation" />: turning the chest away from the
hips while both stay level, then letting the twist unwind through the standing foot.

→ [Ochos](/dancer/steps/ochos/) · [Pivots & Disassociation](/dancer/steps/pivots/)

## The turn

The <Term id="giro" /> is the follower travelling around the leader — forward step, side step,
back step, side step — while the leader pivots in the middle. It is the most useful thing in
tango on a crowded floor, because it lets you keep dancing without needing anywhere to go.

The <Term id="ocho-cortado" /> is its small cousin: an ocho interrupted halfway and sent back
where it came from, landing in the cross. If you learn one "figure" in your first year, learn
this one — it fits in a square metre and it works with every orchestra.

## Words you will hear and do not need yet

<Accordion title="The vocabulary list" tool="toolbox" label="Glossary">

You will hear these in classes, in videos, and at the side of the floor. Knowing what they mean
is useful. Being able to do them is not urgent — none of them belongs on a crowded social floor
in your first year.

- **<Term id="boleo" />** — a pivot stopped and reversed, so the free leg whips out and back.
  Looks decorative; is actually just a change of direction.
- **<Term id="gancho" />** — a hook, where one dancer's leg wraps briefly around the other's.
  Needs space and trust.
- **<Term id="sacada" />** — a displacement: you step into the space your partner is leaving,
  and their leg appears to be pushed out of the way.
- **Barrida** — a sweep, where one foot appears to drag the other's along the floor.
- **<Term id="adorno" />** — a decoration. A tap, a circle, a brush of the free foot, taken in
  your own time and never at the cost of the beat.
- **Salida** — literally "the exit", confusingly used for the opening steps of a dance or of a
  teaching sequence.

</Accordion>

<Callout type="warning" title="Sequences are not the point">
You will be taught sequences — step here, then here, then a cross. They are a teaching device:
a way of putting several principles in a row so you can feel them. Learning the sequence and
not the principle means you own one thing. Learning the principle means you own everything it
can be combined with.
</Callout>

## How much of this do you need to social dance?

Less than you think. A walk, a pause, a weight change, the cross, and an ocho cortado is a
genuinely complete dance. Put those with the beat and a comfortable embrace and you will be
welcome at any milonga in the world.

<Callout type="question">
Pick the movement on this page that feels worst right now. Is the problem the movement — or is
it your balance, your embrace, or the fact that you cannot hear the beat underneath it? It is
almost always one of the three.
</Callout>
