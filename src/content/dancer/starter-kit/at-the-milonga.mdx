A milonga is a social tango dance, and it runs on conventions that nobody announces. They are
not snobbery — they exist so that a room full of strangers can dance together without
negotiating everything out loud. Here is the whole system.

## How the night is organised

Music comes in <Term id="tanda">tandas</Term>: three or four songs by the same orchestra, in
the same style. You dance a whole tanda with the same person. Between tandas comes a
<Term id="cortina" /> — a short burst of non-tango music, often something deliberately
un-danceable. The cortina means: thank your partner, and clear the floor.

<Callout type="tip" title="Why it works">
The cortina is a graceful exit built into the evening. Nobody has to extract themselves from
anything — the music does it for you. This is why walking off mid-tanda is a strong signal,
and why you should not do it unless something is genuinely wrong.
</Callout>

## Asking, and being asked

Most milongas use the <Term id="cabeceo" />: you invite someone with eye contact and a nod,
from where you are sitting, and they accept with a nod or decline by looking away.

<Accordion title="How the cabeceo actually works" tool="toolbox" label="How to" open>

1. Wait for the tanda to start so everyone knows what is playing.
2. Look around the room. Find the person you want to dance with.
3. Catch their eye and hold it for a moment. Raise your eyebrows, tilt your head.
4. If they nod back, *then* stand up and walk over.
5. If they look away, look somewhere else. That is the entire refusal, and it costs nobody
   anything.

The genius of it is that a decline is invisible. Nobody has been rejected in front of the
room, and nobody had to say no out loud.

</Accordion>

<Callout type="warning" title="Common beginner mistakes">
Walking across the floor to ask someone in person — it puts them on the spot in a way the
cabeceo is specifically designed to avoid. And standing up *before* the nod, which means you
are now walking towards someone who has not agreed to anything.
</Callout>

## On the floor

<KeyPoints title="Floor craft">

- Travel counter-clockwise along the <Term id="line-of-dance" />. Everybody, always.
- Do not overtake. If the couple ahead is slow, dance smaller.
- Do not back up into the person behind you.
- Leaders: you are responsible for what is behind your partner. They cannot see it.
- Keep steps small and low when it is crowded. Save the big movements for an empty floor.
- If you bump someone, make eye contact and acknowledge it. That is all it takes.

</KeyPoints>

<Callout type="remark" title="Lanes">
Busy milongas form lanes — an outer lane that travels steadily and an inner one that is
usually slower and more crowded. Pick a lane and stay in it. Wandering between lanes is how
collisions happen.
</Callout>

## Small things that matter

- Arrive having showered. Bring a spare shirt if you sweat.
- Thank your partner at the end of the tanda. Walk them off the floor if that is the local habit.
- If you are learning, say so. Nobody minds a beginner who is calm and on the beat.
- Watch for a while before you dance. It is the fastest way to learn what this particular room does.

<Callout type="question">
Every milonga has slightly different customs. What does *this* room do — cabeceo or verbal
invitation, three-song or four-song tandas, does anybody sit out the cortina? Ten minutes of
watching answers all of it.
</Callout>
