Welcome. Tango has a reputation for being hard to start, and it is half deserved: the barrier
is not the steps, it is that nothing you already know about dancing transfers cleanly. The
good news is that the list of things that actually matter early on is very short.

<KeyPoints title="If you do nothing else">

- Go regularly. Once a week beats an intensive weekend every three months.
- Work on your walk and your balance, not on figures.
- Dance with people better than you, and with people worse than you.
- Listen to tango music when you are not dancing.

</KeyPoints>

## What to expect

**Months 1–3.** You will feel clumsy and you will count out loud in your head. Everyone did.
Your goal is not to look good; it is to be able to walk with someone to the music without
either of you having to fix anything.

**Months 3–6.** Things start connecting. You will get your first dance that felt like
something, and then spend a month trying to reproduce it.

**Months 6–12.** You start hearing the music instead of just hearing that music is playing.
This is usually when people either fall in love with tango or quietly stop.

<Callout type="warning" title="The trap">
Collecting steps. It is tempting, because steps are countable and progress feels visible. But
a dancer with three movements and a good walk is a much better partner than one with thirty
movements and no balance — and everyone on the floor can tell the difference immediately.
</Callout>

## What to work on

<Accordion title="The short list" tool="toolbox" label="Priorities" open>

1. **Balance.** Can you stand on one foot, calmly, for a minute?
2. **The walk.** Slow, complete steps, arriving on the beat.
3. **The embrace.** Comfortable for both of you, held rather than clamped.
4. **The beat.** Can you find it and stay on it for a whole track?
5. **Floor craft.** Not running into anyone is a genuine skill and it is appreciated.

Everything else can wait a year without any harm at all.

</Accordion>

<Callout type="tip" title="Learn both roles if you can">
Even a little. Following teaches leaders what their lead actually feels like from the other
side, which is information you cannot get any other way, and leading teaches followers what is
and is not being asked for.
</Callout>

## What about shoes?

For the first few months: smooth-soled shoes that stay on your feet and let you pivot. That is
the whole requirement. Proper tango shoes are wonderful and you should get some eventually,
but they will not make you dance better in month two.

<Callout type="question">
Before your next class, ask yourself: what is the one thing I want to be able to do by the end
of it? Going in with a question gets you more than going in hoping to be taught something.
</Callout>
