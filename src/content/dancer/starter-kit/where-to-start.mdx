Tango has a reputation for being hard to start, and it is half deserved: the barrier is not
the steps, it is that nothing you already know about dancing transfers cleanly. The good news
is that the list of things that actually matter early on is very short, and none of it is a
figure with a Spanish name.

<KeyPoints title="If you do nothing else">

- Go regularly. Once a week beats an intensive weekend every three months.
- Work on your walk and your balance, not on figures.
- Dance with people better than you, and with people worse than you.
- Listen to tango music when you are not dancing.

</KeyPoints>

## The four pillars

Everything you will ever work on in tango sits on one of four pillars. Classes tend to teach
across all of them at once without saying so, which is part of why the early months feel
scattered. Knowing which pillar a given frustration belongs to is most of the fix.

### 1. Your own body

Balance, posture, and the ability to move your own weight from one foot to the other with
control. This is the pillar everyone wants to skip, and it is the one that determines how
every other pillar feels.

**What good looks like:** you can stand on one foot, calmly, for a minute. You can walk slowly
and stop mid-step without a wobble. Your partner cannot feel you catching yourself.

**How to work on it:** alone, at home, in the kitchen, without music. Ten minutes of weight
changes is worth more than an hour of watching. → [Drills for the body](/dancer/drills/#body) ·
[Posture & Axis](/dancer/steps/posture/) · [The Walk](/dancer/steps/walk/) ·
[Practice at Home](/dancer/resources/practice-at-home/)

<Callout type="remark" title="Why this comes first">
Almost every problem beginners bring to a teacher — "my ochos stall", "the cross feels
horrible", "they keep losing me" — is a balance problem wearing a costume. Fix the cause, not
the symptom.
</Callout>

### 2. Connection

The <Term id="abrazo" />, and the two-way communication that runs through it. Leading is not
pushing and following is not guessing; both are ways of paying attention. This is the pillar
that decides whether people want to dance with you again.

**What good looks like:** your embrace is comfortable for both of you, held rather than
clamped. You can tell what your partner is doing without looking. You are not arriving
somewhere before they do.

**How to work on it:** with a partner, slowly, at a <Term id="practica" />, and by asking what
things felt like rather than telling each other what to do. →
[Drills for connection](/dancer/drills/#connection) · [The Embrace](/dancer/connection/embrace/) ·
[Leading & Following](/dancer/connection/leading-and-following/)

### 3. The music

Hearing the beat, then the phrase, then the difference between one orchestra and another.
Tango music is not a backing track — it is the thing you are both listening to, and it is what
turns two people executing movements into two people dancing.

**What good looks like:** you can find the beat within a few seconds of a track starting and
stay on it for the whole song. You can hear a phrase ending before it ends.

**How to work on it:** listening, mostly, when you are not dancing. In the car, cooking,
walking. → [Drills for the music](/dancer/drills/#music) · [The Beat](/dancer/musicality/the-beat/) ·
[Phrasing](/dancer/musicality/phrasing/) · [Knowing the Orchestras](/dancer/musicality/orchestras/)

### 4. The floor

Navigation, the <Term id="ronda" />, and the social craft of a milonga — inviting, accepting,
travelling without collisions, being pleasant to share a floor with. Nobody teaches this in a
beginner class and everybody notices whether you have it.

**What good looks like:** you can dance a whole tanda without overtaking anyone, backing into
anyone, or having to look over your shoulder in alarm.

**How to work on it:** by going to milongas, watching for a while, and dancing smaller than
you think you need to. → [Drills for the floor](/dancer/drills/#floor) ·
[Your Milonga Survival Guide](/dancer/starter-kit/milonga-survival-guide/)

<Callout type="warning" title="The fifth pillar that is not one">
Vocabulary — figures, sequences, the movements with names. It feels like the main event because
it is the countable part, and it is the last thing that matters. A dancer with three movements
and a good walk is a far better partner than one with thirty movements and no balance, and
everyone on the floor can tell the difference immediately.
</Callout>

## What to expect

**Months 1–3.** You will feel clumsy and you will count out loud in your head. Everyone did.
Your goal is not to look good; it is to be able to walk with someone to the music without
either of you having to fix anything.

**Months 3–6.** Things start connecting. You will get your first dance that felt like
something, and then spend a month trying to reproduce it.

**Months 6–12.** You start hearing the music instead of just hearing that music is playing.
This is usually when people either fall in love with tango or quietly stop.

<Callout type="note" title="Progress is not linear">
Tango improves in steps, not slopes. You will spend six weeks feeling like nothing is changing
and then have one night where something obviously works. The flat weeks are not wasted; they
are where the work is being absorbed.
</Callout>

## How to spend the first year

<Accordion title="The short list, in priority order" tool="toolbox" label="Priorities" open>

1. **Balance.** Can you stand on one foot, calmly, for a minute?
2. **The walk.** Slow, complete steps, arriving on the beat.
3. **The embrace.** Comfortable for both of you, held rather than clamped.
4. **The beat.** Can you find it and stay on it for a whole track?
5. **Floor craft.** Not running into anyone is a genuine skill, and it is appreciated.

Everything else can wait a year without any harm at all.

</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Choosing classes and teachers" tool="toolbox" label="Practical">

**Go weekly, to the same class.** Consistency beats intensity by a wide margin. One class a
week for a year will take you much further than four workshop weekends.

**Go to the second class.** The first one is disorienting for everybody. The second is where
it starts making sense, and it is the one most people never come back for.

**Add a practica before you add a second class.** Class gives you information faster than you
can absorb it; the practica is where any of it gets into your body.

**Find a teacher who explains why.** If every answer is "just do it like this", you will hit a
ceiling. Good teachers hand you principles you can apply to things they never showed you.

**Do not shop around too early.** Different teachers use contradictory language for the same
thing, and in month two you have no way to reconcile it. Pick one and stay a while.

</Accordion>

<Callout type="warning" title="The trap">
Collecting steps. It is tempting, because steps are countable and progress feels visible. But
the collection does not compound — the pillars do. Ask yourself after a class whether you got
better at something or merely learned another thing.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Learn both roles if you can">
Even a little. Following teaches leaders what their lead actually feels like from the other
side, which is information you cannot get any other way, and leading teaches followers what is
and is not being asked for.
</Callout>

## What about shoes?

For the first few months: smooth-soled shoes that stay on your feet and let you pivot. That is
the whole requirement. Leather or suede soles are ideal; rubber grips the floor and will fight
every pivot you attempt. Proper tango shoes are wonderful and you should get some eventually,
but they will not make you dance better in month two.

When you do buy: fit matters more than heel height, and a lower heel is not a beginner
compromise — plenty of excellent dancers never leave one.

<Callout type="question">
Before your next class, ask yourself: what is the one thing I want to be able to do by the end
of it? Going in with a question gets you more than going in hoping to be taught something.
</Callout>
